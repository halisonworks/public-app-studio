About & background

For centuries Europe used the Julian calendar (the Old Style, or OS). Because the Julian year is slightly too long, the calendar slowly drifted out of step with the seasons. The Gregorian calendar (the New Style, or NS) corrected this. Catholic countries adopted it in 1582, but Britain and its colonies held out until September 1752.

When Britain switched, it had to drop 11 days to catch up: Wednesday 2 September 1752 was immediately followed by Thursday 14 September 1752. No days went missing physically — only the labels changed.

Separately, England's civil year began on 25 March (Lady Day), not 1 January, until 1752. So a date such as 11 February that we would call 1732 was, at the time, still written as part of 1731. To avoid confusion, historians and registers often double date these dates between 1 January and 24 March — for example 11 February 1731/32.

For understanding historical records. This tool uses one consistent Julian Day Number engine and the proleptic Julian and Gregorian calendars (extending each backward beyond its real-world adoption). Different regions changed over on different dates, so always interpret a source against the calendar actually in use where and when it was written. Years are treated as astronomical-style continuous numbers; there is no year 0 in the historical record, so take great care with dates around 1 BC / AD 1.
