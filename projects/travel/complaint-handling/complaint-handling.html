Why it matters

Complaints are opportunities

Studies show that a customer whose complaint is handled well can become more loyal than a customer who never had a problem at all. This is called the service recovery paradox. Your response in the first 30 seconds often determines the outcome.

The LAST Model

A four-step de-escalation framework used in hospitality worldwide.

L

Listen

Give the customer your full attention. Let them finish without interrupting. Nod. Use their name. Your body language signals that you take them seriously.

A

Acknowledge & Apologise

Validate their feelings first, then apologise for the inconvenience — even if it was not directly your fault. "I completely understand how frustrating this must be."

S

Solve

Offer a concrete remedy. Where possible, give the customer a choice between two or more options — this restores their sense of control. Commit to a timeline.

T

Thank

Thank the customer for bringing it to your attention. This reinforces that feedback is welcome and turns a negative ending into a positive one.

De-escalation Principles

Your tone sets the ceiling for the conversation. Speak slowly and softly. If you feel defensive, breathe before answering. A calm voice is contagious — most customers will mirror it within a minute.

"That's not our policy" or "It's not my fault" are de-escalation killers. Even if the customer is wrong, defending the institution first makes them feel dismissed. Acknowledge first, explain later — and only briefly.

Emotions must be addressed before logic can land. "I hear how frustrated you are" opens a door; "Actually, the booking clearly states…" slams it. Acknowledge first, then move to facts and solutions.

Say "I will sort this out" rather than "Someone will call you back." Taking personal responsibility — even for systemic issues — signals to the customer that a real person cares. If you need to escalate, stay the point of contact.

A follow-up — even a brief one — transforms "resolved" into "impressed." Check back within the agreed timeframe, confirm the fix worked, and ask if anything else is needed. This is often what converts a complainer into a loyal regular.

Conflict Prevention

Most complaints stem from unmet expectations — not bad service. Prevention is better than cure.

Set clear expectations

Tell guests what's included, what's not, check-in times, cancellation terms — proactively, at booking, and at arrival.

Communicate early

If something will go wrong (delay, shortage, change) — call/message before the customer finds out on their own. Control the narrative.

Invite feedback

Guests who feel heard are less likely to escalate. A simple "Is everything to your satisfaction?" at mid-stay catches problems while they're still fixable.

The Service Recovery Paradox

Research (Hart, Heskett & Sasser, 1990) found that customers who experience a service failure but receive an excellent recovery often report higher satisfaction and loyalty than customers who never had a problem. A complaint handled brilliantly is free marketing.

When to Escalate

Situation Action
Minor complaint, within your authority to fixHandle yourself — don't pass it on unnecessarily
Compensation or refund above your limitInvolve a supervisor; stay present and introduce them
Customer is verbally abusive or threateningCalmly involve security/manager; safety first
Situation involves legal liability or press riskEscalate immediately; don't speculate or promise
Emergency (medical, fire, safety)Follow emergency protocol; call services first, service second
